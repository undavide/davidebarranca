
As I'm about to pivot away from Photoshop post-production to... a _different job_ (juicer details in a future post), I'd like to spend some time revisiting my almost quarter-century-long career pushing pixels around. Although this blog has mostly been software development-centric, I've injected *pure* Photoshop content here and there over the years, sometimes [directly hinting](https://www.davidebarranca.com/2012/01/post-production-in-the-contemporary-art-photography-business-details/) at what I've invariably considered my daytime job.
Now that this surprisingly long-lived era is ending, I feel compelled to write a very loose account of how I got into this business and how it played out as a way to pay tribute to all the hours I've filled either with a Wacom pen in my hand, tucking paper rolls into a large format Epson printer, or trying to solve the wank of the day in the studio.

## An unplanned career

The fact is that I should have been an Industrial Chemist; that's what my high school diploma says and what my degree would have been if I hadn't dropped out of university after four years. Besides the fact that it quite literally stinks, I didn't mind Chemistry at all; but I had had enough. 

In my early twenties, an old friend handed me a book he bought and quickly deemed "utterly boring": it was the Photoshop 5 edition of the Photoshop Bible (circa 1998), which I found oddly fascinating.[^1] Back then, I had a job in a movie theater: low pay and long hours of *ennui* punctuated with peaks of total frenzy. While an assorted nightlife fauna tried (successfully) to make our work time memorable in the wrong way, I managed to spend several hours reading on the lobby's sofas between shows and glancing (unsuccessfully) at the other cashier on whom I had a crush—a girl my age who's now a GP. That Photoshop thing was captivating, though!
One day, loitering in my town's bookstore—as one would in the 1990s—I ran into a book titled "Professional Photoshop" by Dan Margulis, which I immediately bought. Its scope was much narrower than the Bible, covering only color correction but to an incredible depth. I was hooked; I flipped its pages to consumption, trying to digest those alien concepts.
The chief projectionist spotted me, a guy a bit older than me who generally minded his own business. He doubled as a photographer, working for an agency that selected wanna-be models, and producing each month a metric tonne of color prints of next-door-looking people. But he fancied himself as an artist. He had a talent for B&W and an entrepreneurial spirit, but he lacked experience with emerging digital technologies. Having been around computers since I was a kid, with this Margulis theory seeping through my brain, we decided to get into business together. We worked for a bizarre category of clients who would later become very familiar to me: Artists. Some of them really were, and some just had the money and *elan* to play the part. Regardless, they all set me up for a career in the industry.

## Learning the ropes

I tried to absorb as much as possible in the first years, the dawn of the Internet era. Information was more precious than gear; gear itself was pretty darn expensive, and we had nearly no funds. But we poured our hearts into the craft and managed to grow a solid reputation. I also got a side job in a professional photo lab, learning both digital and analog processes. I sneaked in extra retouching for random people's holiday snapshots—which I weirdly enjoyed, to the chagrin of the lab owner who instead wanted me to be just quick-quick! 

In Italy, we were at an inflection point. The rising digital wave was about to crush the analog industry: wedding, portrait, food, sports events, you name it. I witnessed clever operators outsmart seasoned professionals, businesses bet and grow, or try to catch up and fail. My last photo lab wage was paid in kind: I got home with a used Imacon (now Hasselblad) scanner, an unexpected treasure my cinema colleague and I would put to good use in the following many years. I became an expert scanner operator, a skill essential to my career. Running out of business, the photo lab owner asked us if we wanted to take over; we were naive, yet we instinctively got that digital called for leaner operations—not the kind that involved chemicals waste management or warehouse leases. We were competitive in a high-quality niche from a (very cliché) basement garage. 

### Dodging bullets in the saloon

Meanwhile, the Italian scene was a wild west of consultants trying to ~rip off~ sell whatever bit of hardware, software, or pure scam that would theoretically grant the tiniest advantage over a growing lot of competitors. We did really feel like living inside a Petri dish of aggressive peers in a rapidly changing scenario. 
I remember a well-esteemed former university professor who gave us one of the first (very expensive) lessons in digital Color Management. Gold, yet the answer was blunt when we timidly asked about actual ways to implement the theory: "I don't know; I've never really bothered to try."
We were scammed with ICC profiles that, according to another consultant, should have precisely controlled the output of individual ink cartridges while using an RGB driver (a ludicrous proposition) for a Canon printer tweaked to use one of the very first versions of the otherwise excellent Piezography quad-tone inks. If you're old enough, we got the infamous Museum Black that quickly turned into funny, dark indigo.
We were one of the first print rooms in Italy to use Hahnemühle cotton rag-based paper. We would have started even earlier: we heard about them but weren't sure about the German brand name spelling—googling wasn't very effective back then! Once, we misplaced an order and were shipped a hundred A0 paper sheets (about 84x120cm) in a wooden crate. When asked about the coating's metamerism, a Hahnemüle Italian salesman joked about *meteorism*.
We bought one of the first consumer-oriented X-Rite spectrophotometers; another well-known consultant delivered the box to our garage/studio. For once, he was a laid-back, friendly guy who used to drive a Porsche, play the electric guitar, surround himself with cats, and shoot boudoir pictures. Sadly, he died in his early fifties of cancer. With our brains oozing color management theory, we were eager to crack the nut: "How do we use it?" we asked. "Oh, don't worry about the theory, it's super easy; there's a CD-ROM inside with the instructions, just follow them." I did, and in no time, I was profiling our color printer and calibrating grey curves on an actual quad-tone RIP.
There was a Photoshop plugin whose pushy marketers claimed to implement a NASA-based algorithm (whose name is now copyrighted as a dietary supplement, of all things) used to enhance satellite pictures for military purposes—utter bullshit, obviously. It was sold to pre-press houses at ludicrous prices. Years later, when the hype faded, I found an old cracked version and managed to (mostly) reverse-engineer it. It was nothing but a clever Automation Plugin that made a fortune back then—unthinkable nowadays.

### Building the foundations

In the mid-2000s, I outgrew my position and quit my first partner in crime. While visiting a trade fair, I got a business card from the founder of a color management consultancy business. He needed an assistant, and I was eager to try new things. For a couple of years, I worked for him, traveled through northern Italy, and consulted for large companies: press and pre-press shops, publishing firms, industrial photo labs, ceramic industries, and the like. I got to work with worryingly expensive equipment in high-stakes production environments. Stressful, technically challenging, but rewarding. I wasn't a Photoshop operator anymore, but I sometimes worked in contexts that employed them—I was looking at my tribe from the other side of the fence. 
And felt for them. For instance, when I was consulting for this ginormous ceramic tiles company, which literally bought part of a town in my region to accommodate its growing business. Street numbers were still posted to their office buildings, between open-air storage areas filled to the brim. They started using digital printing for some of their production lines, and I was working on color fidelity with their design team lead. At the time, they were creating these giant, multichannel files of seamless wooden textures, which were then cut into a number of tiles large enough to avoid repetitions when installed. While discussing the whole process with him—from the moment the ceramic ink hits the tile to the firing, cooling, etc.—I saw a funny old man walking in. He was wearing a pair of aviator glasses with distinctively bright Barbie-pink lenses. He started evaluating the designs and colors in the display area, asking for corrections and such. Everyone treated the man, who obviously was the mighty company owner, with reverence and noted his remarks. As soon as I turned my head towards my guy in total disbelief, he raised his hand and stopped me: "I know. I know."

My ties with Photoshop post-production weren't totally cut, though. Besides writing magazine articles and books, Dan Margulis offered full-immersion classes that went by the name of "Applied Color Theory", or ACT.
